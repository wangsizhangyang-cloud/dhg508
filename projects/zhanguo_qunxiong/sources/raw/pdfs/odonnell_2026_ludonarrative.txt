1. Introduction 
Traditional history education frequently privileges cognitive understanding — the mastery of 
events, motivations, and consequences — while systematically neglecting the affective 
dimension of historical experience. Yet historical empathy, as Endacott and Brooks [1] 
demonstrate, is constitutively dual: it requires both cognitive contextualisation and affective 
connection to the lived experiences of historical actors. Without the latter, students may 
accumulate factual knowledge of atrocity whilst remaining emotionally insulated from its 
human cost.

The paper's advancement over the existing state of the art is twofold. First, whilst game-
based learning (GBL) scholarship has extensively documented cognitive outcomes of historical 
games [3]–[6], comparatively little work isolates the affective mechanisms and design features 
through which empathy — particularly its emotional, felt dimension — is generated. Second, 
the paper situates this analysis within Ambrosio's HSBG framework, extending it to specify 
the design conditions under which ludonarrative consonance (the purposeful alignment of 
procedural mechanics and historical narrative) produces affective rather than merely cognitive 
engagement. 
Preliminary empirical grounding for these design-analytical claims is provided by a 
companion study [7] examining participant perceptions of the game across a 65-person 
questionnaire sample. That study found that 87.7% of respondents agreed that simulation-based 
approaches are appropriate for teaching sensitive historical events, and 96.9% stressed the 
importance of including all factional voices. These findings provide supporting empirical 
context for the design-analytical claims advanced here, whilst the limitations of that study — 
a predominantly male hobbyist gaming sample (92.3% male) with fewer than 5% of 
participants from Ireland — underline the need for the pedagogically-grounded design analysis 
this paper offers. The current paper makes no claim about what specific populations will feel 
or understand as a result of playing the game; it evaluates the design conditions under which 
affective empathy mechanisms are operationalised. Future empirical studies examining player 
responses in educational settings would constitute a complementary research contribution 
building on this foundation.

2. Literature Review 
 
2.1 
Historical Empathy: Cognitive and Affective Dimensions 
The study of actors from the past requires situating them within the societal, geopolitical, and 
ideological structures that shaped their lives [8]. Historical empathy provides the 
epistemological framework for this task. Endacott and Brooks [1] establish the field's 
foundational distinction: cognitive empathy — the intellectual understanding of another's 
perspective, motivations, and constraints — and affective empathy — the emotional resonance 
with another's lived experience. These are complementary but distinct. Cognitive empathy 
enables critical analysis; affective empathy enables the felt connection that makes history 
personally meaningful. 
This paper is primarily concerned with the latter. Affective empathy is the dimension 
most frequently underdeveloped in history education [9], [10] and the dimension most 
theoretically tractable in game design, where the mechanic — the rule system a player inhabits 
— can simulate not merely the facts of a historical constraint but its experience. 
 
2.2 
Game-Based Learning and Historical Games 
Game-based learning (GBL) is an educational approach using games as pedagogical tools to 
promote learning through engagement, problem-solving, and critical thinking [5], [6]. Unlike 
traditional methods, games provide interactive environments in which students step into the 
roles of historical actors, make decisions, and encounter the consequences of those choices. 
Historical Game Studies (HGS), as theorised by Chapman [3], [4], examines how games serve 
as media for conveying historical knowledge and empathy. Chapman argues that historical 
games have the capacity to engage players in critical thinking while enabling a visceral, 
emotionally veridical experience of the past — a "lived" encounter with history that textbooks 
and documentaries cannot provide. Through interactive mechanics such as role-playing and 
decision-making, players can experience the first-hand consequences of historical actions. 
Research on empathy in historical games has been substantial [11]–[13]. Studies of titles such 
as Freedom: The Underground Railroad and Twilight Struggle demonstrate that games not only 
transmit historical knowledge but evoke emotional responses that facilitate personal connection 
with the past. The more immersive and emotionally engaging a game is, the more likely it is to 
foster deeper empathy [14], [15]. However, this scholarship rarely distinguishes the specific 
design features — the mechanics, visuals, and materials — through which affective (as opposed 
to cognitive) empathy is generated. The present paper addresses this gap. 
  
2.3 
Historically-Structured Boardgames 
Historically-structured boardgames (HSBGs), as defined by Ambrosio [2], immerse players 
within a "heuristic cycle in which imagination is informed by evidence" [8]. These games are 
designed to place players within the social and political context of a historical conflict, where 
choices are shaped by the documented structures of that environment. This distinguishes 
HSBGs from games that use historical settings as backdrops for entertainment mechanics: in 
an HSBG, the mechanics are the historiographical argument [16]. 
Ambrosio's framework emphasises that cognitive and affective dimensions of empathy 
are not incidental by-products of play but intentionally cultivated through the entire design 
architecture — the mechanics, narrative, visual components, and physical objects that together

affective mechanisms activated by each design layer in a specific HSBG. 
 
2.4 
Narrative, Visual Representation, and Physical Components 
Narratives humanise history, transforming abstract events into personal stories that evoke 
emotional responses [17]–[19]. In HSBGs, narrative structures provide the context for players 
to immerse themselves in the lives and perspectives of historical individuals, enabling the 
emotional resonance that cognitive engagement alone cannot produce [20], [21]. The concept 
of "narrative agency" [22] — the capacity of players to make choices that shape the story — 
amplifies empathic investment, as players internalise the consequences of decisions that bear 
on the lives of historical others. 
Visual representations — particularly images of fatalities, violence, and suffering — 
powerfully influence both cognitive and affective empathy [23]. Period-authentic imagery 
grounds players in historical reality rather than aestheticised reconstruction, resisting the 
distancing effect of abstraction. 
The tangible elements of a game — maps, boards, cards, wooden and plastic components 
— play a crucial role in shaping affective engagement [24]. The physical act of handling game 
pieces can evoke emotional reactions unavailable through digital representation: removing a 
token representing a person personalises loss; placing a casualty marker makes the human cost 
of a decision physically present. As Pearce observes, objects "provoke certain reactions and 
expectations which we project back on to [them]" [24, p. 220], deepening players' connection 
to the history those objects represent. 
3. Methods and Material 
This paper employs a qualitative design analysis methodology, examining The Troubles as a 
designed artefact intended to produce specific affective outcomes. The analytical framework 
draws on two established bodies of work: Ambrosio's model of historically-structured 
boardgames [2], [16], [25], which provides criteria for evaluating how game mechanics 
operationalise historical structures; and Endacott and Brooks's dual cognitive–affective model 
of historical empathy [1], which specifies the psychological conditions for each empathic 
dimension. 
The analysis proceeds across three levels corresponding to the game's principal design 
layers: (1) narrative structure, examined through the game's 260 narrative event cards and 
accompanying historical playbook; (2) visual design, examined through the imagery deployed 
in cards, components, and board art; and (3) physical and material components, examined 
through the game's tactile elements and their role in producing embodied historical 
engagement. At each level, the analysis asks: What affective empathy mechanisms are 
activated by this design feature, and what documented psychological processes account for 
those effects? 
Three forms of triangulation supplement the design analysis. First, expert elicitation: 
the game's artist, Domhnall Hegarty, provided direct commentary on design intentions for the 
visual and aesthetic dimensions, enabling the analysis to attend to intended as well as observed 
effects. Second, a structured comparative analysis situates The Troubles against six comparable 
serious and historical games (see Table 1), establishing the game's distinctive contribution to 
the HSBG genre. Third, selected findings from a companion empirical study [7] — a 65-
participant questionnaire examining perceptions of the game among hobbyist gamers — 
provide preliminary empirical context for the design-analytical claims advanced at each stage 
of the analysis. 
Consistent with Ambrosio's framework [2], the analysis does not evaluate player outcomes 
empirically: this paper makes no claim about what specific populations will feel or understand

3.1 
Case Selection: the Troubles 
 
3.1.1 
Historical Context 
The period of conflict that engulfed Northern Ireland between 1969 and 1998 is commonly 
known as the Troubles. Its origins can be traced to the sectarian divisions that emerged 
following the partition of Ireland in 1922, when six of Ireland's thirty-two counties were 
incorporated into the United Kingdom while the remaining twenty-six counties became the 
Irish Free State. 
Between 1922 and 1969, Northern Ireland operated under a Unionist-controlled electoral 
system widely regarded as structurally disadvantaging the Nationalist, predominantly Catholic 
population (see Figure 2) [27]. 
  
 
 
Figure 2. Map of 1966 Westminster Election Results in Northern Ireland: Unionists dominate 
Nationalist MPs (11–1). Source: ARK Northern Ireland, 2025. 
 
Although Unionist Prime Minister Sir Terence O'Neill sought reform amid the rise of the 
civil rights movement, his measures provoked a resurgence of Loyalist paramilitarism. In 
August 1969, as sectarian violence escalated, British troops were deployed — what became the 
longest military deployment in British history (see Figure 3). The conflict resulted in 
approximately 3,500 direct fatalities, with an estimated 24,000 additional deaths attributed to 
structural violence [26].

For historical events of this magnitude and political sensitivity, affective empathy is an 
essential pedagogical goal. Cognitive empathy enables students to grasp the competing 
ideological stances of the conflict's factions; affective empathy enables them to engage 
emotionally with the personal toll of violence, loss, and division. 
 
3.1.2 
The Game  
The Troubles: Shadow War in Northern Ireland is a forthcoming card-driven game (CDG) 
published by Compass Games (see Figure 4). 
 
 
  
Figure 4. Iconic image of the 'Boy in the Gas Mask'. Source: The Troubles: Shadow War in 
Northern Ireland. Reprinted with permission from Compass Games LLC. 
 
Designed by Hugh O'Donnell and illustrated by Domhnall Hegarty, the game simulates the 
conflict through competing factions — British Forces, the Ulster Defence Regiment, the Royal 
Ulster Constabulary, the IRA, Loyalist paramilitaries, and civilian and political organisations 
— each pursuing asymmetric objectives within a shared historical environment. Play is 
structured around 260 narrative event cards, each representing actual historical episodes across 
nine chronological scenarios ('Epochs') spanning 1964–1998. 
The game is selected as the primary case for three reasons. First, it exemplifies Ambrosio's 
HSBG design philosophy [2]: its mechanics are derived from historical structures rather than 
imposed as entertainment features. Second, it addresses a contested history characterised by 
living memory, ongoing community sensitivity, and structural violence that demands affective 
rather than merely cognitive engagement. Third, it provides sufficient design complexity — 
across narrative, visual, and material dimensions — for a multi-layered analysis of affective 
empathy mechanisms.

4. Results 
4.1 
Narrative as Affective Mechanism 
4.1.1 
Card-Driven Narrative and Ludonarrative Consonance 
Released in 1993 and designed by Mark Herman, We the People is widely regarded as the first 
wargame to incorporate the card-driven mechanic, in which cards both support and direct 
player actions through historically-framed events. The Troubles adopts and deepens this 
mechanic: its 260 Narrative Event Cards are the primary vehicle through which historical 
reality enters gameplay. 
Each card represents an actual episode in the Northern Ireland conflict's timeline — ranging 
from the strategic and geopolitical to the domestic and deeply personal. Most provide 
historically accurate instructions for the executing faction, while some enable a Temporary 
Effect that mimics a historical event, alters player capabilities, or modifies victory conditions. 
Cards are grouped into nine Epochs, each covering a distinct chronological period; the full 
sequence of all 260 cards, played in order, constitutes the complete game (see Figure 5). 
 
 
 
Figure 5. Overview of the nine Epochs into which the Narrative Cards are divided; 'The 
Troubles' is the use of all 260 cards played in chronological order. Source: Scenario table from 
Rulebook, The Troubles: Shadow War in Northern Ireland. Reprinted with permission from 
Compass Games LLC. 
 
This design creates what this paper terms ludonarrative consonance — the purposeful 
alignment of mechanical action and historical meaning. Where ludonarrative dissonance [28] 
describes the mismatch between a game's mechanical logic and its narrative claims, consonance 
describes the condition in which the two are mutually reinforcing. Playing a Narrative Event 
Card in The Troubles is not merely a strategic action: it is the enactment of a historical event 
whose consequences reverberate through the game system. Players co-construct an emergent 
narrative [25], [29] that is simultaneously their own and historically grounded. 
The accompanying Historical Playbook provides expanded textual narratives for each card, 
enabling players to situate each mechanic within its documented historical context. This 
paratextual dimension ensures that historical authenticity is not merely aesthetic but accessible: 
players are, as Hegarty notes: 
"under no illusion that they are dealing with anything other than actual events, many of which 
involved atrocities and the fatalities of approximately two thousand innocent human beings" 
[30].

4.2 
Visual Representation as Affective Mechanism 
4.2.1 
Period-Authentic Imagery 
The visual design of The Troubles is the work of artist Domhnall Hegarty, whose prior credits 
include political and wargame titles such as 1960: The Making of a President, Labyrinth, and Red 
Flag Over Paris. The visual approach is explicitly memory-driven: 
 
"I went with a color scheme that most reflects my memories of the troubles. Late 
Seventies through mid-Eighties, the time when I was least consciously aware that there 
was anything out of the norm with the state of things" [30]. 
 
Each of the 260 Narrative Cards features a still image derived from the dominant media of its 
historical period — initially high-contrast black-and-white, graduating to colour with the game's 
later Epochs, mimicking the shift from print journalism to television as the dominant news 
medium (see Figure 6). 
 
 
 
Figure 6. Narrative Card No. 153: Two IRA prisoners depicted undertaking the 'blanket protests' 
in the Maze Prison. Source: The Troubles: Shadow War in Northern Ireland. Reprinted with 
permission from Compass Games LLC. 
 
As Hegarty elaborates: 
 
"The images are meant to evoke the main media of news in my youth — newspapers, with 
TV style and color coming later in the cards. They are meant to be rough and a bit 
fragmentary like many of my memories of the Troubles… Hard black/white contrast like 
the narratives of the sides" [30]. 
 
This period-authenticity is not merely aesthetic: it situates each card's image within the 
perceptual and emotional register in which the Troubles were actually experienced by those who 
lived through them. Players are not viewing a reconstructed or aestheticised conflict; they are 
encountering the visual record of the event itself. 
4.2.2 
Categories of Visual Subject and Their Affective Effects 
The images deployed across the 260 cards and the game's physical components can be analysed 
according to four categories of visual subject, each activating distinct cognitive and affective 
empathy mechanisms.

Civilians. Images depicting civilians — particularly those in positions of vulnerability, suffering, 
or grief — are among the most affectively potent elements of the game's visual design. 
Cognitively, these images foreground the disproportionate impact of political violence on non-
combatants, prompting players to consider the consequences of their factional decisions for those 
with the least agency in the conflict. Affectively, the personalisation effect [38] operates: a 
photograph of a family displaced from their home or an injured civilian in the aftermath of a 
bombing gives "a human face" to abstract casualty statistics, generating sorrow and compassion 
that more generalised representations do not. 
Players who have made decisions that harm civilians encounter these images as a form of 
moral accountability — a visual confrontation with the consequences of strategic choices (see 
Figure 7). This confrontation deepens affective empathy by requiring players to feel the human 
cost of decisions that they made, rather than merely recording their strategic outcome. 
 
 
 
Figure 7. Civilian casualties and prisoners sit alongside British and RUC Forces, IRA and 
Loyalist factions. Source: VASSAL, The Troubles: Shadow War in Northern Ireland. Reprinted 
with permission from Compass Games LLC. 
 
Soldiers. Images of soldiers — both the British Forces and Ulster Defence Regiment who 
represent state authority, and the paramilitaries who contest it — activate a more complex 
affective response. At the cognitive level, soldier imagery prompts reflection on the personal toll 
of military participation: soldiers are individuals caught within large ideological and political 
structures, and images that humanise rather than simply instrumentalise them challenge players to 
extend empathy to those whose roles they may find morally difficult. A soldier mourning a fallen 
colleague may prompt sympathy even from a player opposed to the faction that soldier represents. 
The affective response to soldier imagery is more ambivalent than to civilian imagery, 
and this ambivalence is pedagogically productive: it resists the binary moral simplification of the 
conflict into "good" and "bad" actors, reflecting instead the complex moral landscape that 
historical actors actually inhabited. 
 
Victims and Perpetrators. The visual representation of victims — civilian and military alike — 
activates strong affective responses including grief, anger, and guilt [23]. Images that give 
individual faces and names to casualties resist the psychological mechanism of numerical 
insensitivity [38] that allows mass casualty figures to be processed without emotional impact. 
When a player encounters an image of a specific individual — a named victim of a named 
bombing — the statistical becomes personal. 
Images depicting perpetrators of violence produce a more cognitively demanding form of 
empathy: the capacity to understand motivations and circumstances without endorsing actions. 
This is the form of empathy that Endacott and Brooks [1] identify as most challenging and most 
valuable in historical education. Images that show perpetrators in moments of vulnerability or 
internal conflict create what this paper terms moral dissonance: the unsettling recognition that 
those who inflict harm are also human. 
The practical significance of this visual design is suggested by the companion study's 
finding that 75% of participants viewed simulation engagement as a tool for ethical reflection, 
and 71% agreed that the Troubles involves moral dilemmas not reducible to binary right/wrong 
judgements [7] — a resistance to moral simplification that the game's victim and perpetrator

imagery is specifically designed to cultivate. The finding that over 95% of participants endorsed 
including personal and emotional experiences in history education further supports the role of 
affectively-charged visual content in facilitating the kind of engaged historical learning this 
design analysis theorises. 
 
Paramilitaries. Images of paramilitary fighters and figures labelled as "terrorists" in the 
historical record require the most careful visual and analytical handling. These images challenge 
players to reflect on how ideology, community identity, and historical grievance shape 
participation in political violence, and to resist the temptation to reduce complex actors to simple 
moral categories. The game's visual treatment — placing paramilitary members in moments of 
loss, imprisonment, or everyday life alongside their operational roles — creates the moral 
dissonance through which affective empathy becomes possible even toward actors whose actions 
are indefensible (see Figure 8).

As Bermudez [33, p. 282] argues, narratives that "de-normalize violence expose the agency 
and responsibility of different actors, on different sides of a conflict." The visual design of The 
Troubles performs this de-normalisation: it presents paramilitaries as historical actors with 
comprehensible motivations rather than as monolithic villains, demanding from players an 
empathic effort that is simultaneously intellectually rigorous and emotionally costly. 
 
 
 
4.3 
Physical and Material Components as Affective Mechanism 
4.3.1 
The Game's Material World 
The physical components of The Troubles — its two map-boards, 260 narrative cards, wooden 
cubes, octagonal tokens, military checkpoint markers, and status tables — are not merely the 
mechanical substrate of gameplay. They are material representations of the conflict, and the 
acts of handling, placing, and removing them constitute a form of tactile engagement with 
history that differs fundamentally from reading about it. 
The main playing area depicts the six counties of Northern Ireland — 14,000 square 
kilometres of urban, open, and rural land upon which thousands of lives were lost (see Figure 9). 
A smaller separate insert depicts Belfast in detail. The names on the map — Londonderry 
(Derry), Belfast, Westminster, Dublin, Enniskillen, Omagh — are not merely geographical

Every object in The Troubles, as Leinhardt (in [39]) observes of museum artefacts, maintains a

drawings, and which can evoke stronger empathic responses through its tangibility. 
4.3.2 
Cognitive Empathy through Tactile Engagement 
From a cognitive empathy perspective, the physical manipulation of game components — moving 
wooden cubes, placing military checkpoint tokens, managing resources and funds — encourages 
players to think critically about historical constraints and decision-making. The act of positioning 
figures or drawing narrative event cards helps players grasp the complex interplay of geopolitical 
strategy, violence, and community resilience, making the conflict's historical dynamics 
comprehensible in ways that abstract description cannot achieve. 
The game's asymmetric faction design — each faction's resources, capabilities, and victory 
conditions reflecting its documented historical position — requires players to adopt the cognitive 
disposition of their assigned group. Understanding why a faction acts as it does, even when those 
actions are morally repugnant [40], [37, p. 12], develops the perspective-taking that lies at the 
heart of cognitive empathy. 
The companion study provides supporting evidence for the perspective-taking mechanism 
activated by asymmetric faction design: willingness to assume controversial roles was high 
overall, with 87.7% willing to play British Forces, 86.2% the IRA, and 84.6% Nationalist 
politicians [7]. A noteworthy asymmetry emerged, however: willingness to play Loyalist 
paramilitaries was lower (70.8%), a pattern the study interprets as reflecting pre-existing cultural 
narratives granting republican violence greater perceived international legibility than loyalist 
violence, rather than a failure of the game's design per se. This asymmetry is itself pedagogically 
significant: it demonstrates the game's capacity to surface rather than conceal the interpretive 
frameworks players bring to contested histories — precisely the kind of critical reflexivity that 
HSBG pedagogy aims to cultivate. 
4.3.3 
Affective Empathy through Material Interaction 
The affective dimension of physical interaction is most powerfully activated by the game's 
casualty and imprisonment mechanics. When a player must physically remove a game piece 
representing a person — or place a casualty marker on the board — the act personalises loss in a 
way that abstract casualty tallies do not. The physical removal of a token creates a felt sense of

This mechanism operationalises what Small, Loewenstein and Slovic [38] term the identifiable

3,500 direct fatalities from a number into a sequence of discrete, felt losses. 
The texture, weight, and design of the components also contribute to affective engagement: 
period-appropriate colour schemes, image quality, and font design for narrative event cards create 
a sensory link to the past that enhances immersion. Custom-designed character cards with 
individual biographies encourage players to form attachments to specific individuals within the 
game, amplifying the affective stakes of decisions that affect those individuals. 
4.3.4 
Narrative Mechanisms for Affective Empathy 
The narrative structure of The Troubles activates three documented affective processes. 
Narrative transportation [31], [32]: by positioning each card as an episode within a longer 
historical process, the game's narrative pulls players into the flow of the Troubles' chronology. 
Rather than encountering isolated historical facts, players experience historical time as a 
sequence of episodes in which their decisions bear on what comes next. Bermudez [33, p. 279] 
describes this effect in historical memory education as narrativising "the meaning of a violent 
past by positioning it as an episode of longer historical processes." 
Narrative agency and consequence [22]: players are not passive recipients of the narrative 
but active participants whose choices shape its direction. This agency creates emotional 
investment: when a faction decision leads to civilian casualties, the player experiences the 
consequences not as historical information but as the outcome of their own action. Kaplan, 
Garner, and Brock [34] and Kello and Wagner [35] document how this form of participatory 
consequence-bearing generates stronger affective empathy than passive exposure to historical 
narratives. 
Empirical support for this mechanism is provided by the companion study, in which only 
12.3% of participants reported difficulty empathising with individuals involved in violent 
political conflict, despite just 32.3% believing that violence could be justified — a 20-
percentage-point "empathy gap" suggesting that the game's narrative structure successfully 
cultivates affective connection without endorsing violent action [7]. This pattern directly 
instantiates what Endacott and Brooks [1] identify as the distinguishing condition of mature

Halpern (cited in [10, p. 398]) observes that "when one person truly listens to another's 
story, emotional resonance and empathy often arise naturally." The card-driven narrative 
creates an analogue of this listening — a structured attentiveness to the particularity of each 
historical episode. 
4.3.5 
Ethical Reflection through Physical Components 
The physical components of The Troubles are also vehicles for ethical reflection. When players 
must allocate limited resources using physical tokens — deciding which communities receive 
protection, which operations are sanctioned, which compromises are accepted — the materiality 
of the decision makes its moral weight tangible. This differs from digital decision-making, where 
consequences can feel more detached: the physical act of choosing between different game pieces 
or tokens creates a sense of responsibility that invites reflection on the ethical dimensions of the 
historical choices being simulated. 
 
Symbolism operates throughout the game's material design. A deck of Narrative Cards that 
diminishes as each Epoch progresses represents not merely the passage of time but the exhaustion 
of historical alternatives; the Belfast insert, where violence is densest and civilian casualties most 
concentrated, focuses the player's attention on what is at stake in urban tactical decisions. As 
Pearce [24, p. 220] observes, "the need to decipher gives us the chance both to bring out what is 
in the object and what is in ourselves; it is a dynamic, complex movement which unfolds as time 
passes."

The comparative analysis reveals The Troubles' distinctive contribution: it is the only game in

abstracted version of it. Together, these features constitute the ludonarrative consonance 
through which affective rather than merely cognitive empathy is operationalised. 
5. Discussion 
5.1 
Implications for Game Design 
The analysis yields four transferable design principles for educators and designers working 
with contested and emotionally charged histories.

5.1.1 
Ludonarrative consonance as foundational principle 
Games should ensure that mechanics are derived from historical structures rather than applied 
as narrative decoration. When a player experiences mechanical consequences that authentically 
reflect documented historical patterns — counterinsurgency tactics that deepen rather than 
resolve conflict; civilian casualties that erode rather than build community support — the 
procedural system becomes a historiographical argument. This procedural approach enables 
experiential understanding that propositional pedagogy cannot produce [42]. 
5.1.2 
Asymmetric polyvocality 
The use of asymmetric faction design, in which no faction occupies a position of uncomplicated 
moral authority, is essential for historical honesty in games about multi-party conflicts. As 
Bermudez [33, p. 282] argues, narratives that "de-normalize violence expose the agency and 
responsibility of different actors, on different sides of a conflict." Game designers should 
ensure that each faction's capabilities and constraints reflect the documented structural position 
of the historical group it represents. 
5.1.3 
Period-authentic visual design 
The use of colour schemes and imagery that evoke the media of a specific historical period 
deepens emotional engagement by situating the game's world within the perceptual register in 
which historical actors actually experienced events. Sensitively deployed graphics with non-
gratuitous imagery help "to mitigate some of the weight of the subject matter" [41] while 
maintaining the emotional authenticity that affective empathy requires. 
5.1.4 
Physical representation of human cost 
Designers should ensure that human casualties and suffering are rendered physically present 
through game components, not merely noted as numeric variables. The physical act of placing 
and removing tokens representing individuals activates the identifiable victim effect [38] in 
ways that abstract tallying does not. 
5.2 
Implications for Education 
HSBGs like The Troubles offer significant pedagogical potential for teaching contested 
histories, a claim given preliminary empirical grounding by the companion study in which 
93.8% of participants endorsed the simulation as appropriate for sensitive historical subject 
matter, and over 95% agreed that personal and emotional experiences should be included in 
history education [7]. The companion study also found that 96.9% of participants stressed the 
importance of including all factional voices — an endorsement of the polyvocal design central 
to this paper's analysis. However, this potential is conditional on appropriate educational 
framing, and the companion study's demographic profile — 92.3% male, predominantly 
hobbyist gamers, with fewer than 5% from Ireland — itself illustrates why structured 
pedagogical implementation matters: the populations most likely to use this game in 
educational settings differ substantially from those who participated in that study. 
The following strategies are recommended for classroom use. 
5.2.1 
Pre-game historical scaffolding 
Players must possess sufficient baseline knowledge of the conflict to recognise the game's 
structural constraints as historically grounded rather than arbitrary design features. Educators 
should provide chronological background, introduce the conflict's major factions and their 
documented positions, and establish the political and social context before play begins.

5.2.2 
Structured debriefing 
The most important pedagogical work occurs in the post-game discussion. Educators should 
guide students through three phases: affective acknowledgement (naming what was felt during 
play), structural reflection (connecting game mechanics to historical realities), and contextual 
extension (drawing transferable insights about conflict, power, and human cost). Without this 
facilitated transition from affect to cognition, the game's emotional impact dissipates without 
being converted into historical understanding. 
5.2.3 
Role play and perspective-taking 
Pairing game sessions with structured role-playing exercises — requiring students to adopt the 
perspective of a specific faction or individual before or after play — enhances the capacity to 
hold multiple competing viewpoints simultaneously. 
5.2.4 
Integrating primary sources and media 
Educators can enrich the game's narrative dimension by supplementing gameplay with 
documentaries, primary source documents, and testimonial accounts, enabling students to 
situate the game's episodic cards within the broader historical record. 
5.3 
Limitations and Boundary Conditions 
This paper has several limitations that future research should address. Most fundamentally, the 
analysis is design-analytical rather than empirical: it specifies the conditions under which 
affective empathy mechanisms should operate, not evidence that they do operate for specific 
player populations. Empirical studies measuring player affective and cognitive responses — 
including pre- and post-play assessment instruments, think-aloud protocols during play, and 
qualitative interviews — would be required to validate the design-analytical claims advanced 
here. The companion study [7] provides a first step in this direction, but its convenience sample 
of hobbyist gamers is not a pedagogical population, and its findings should be treated as 
indicative rather than confirmatory. 
The paper's conclusions should also be understood as bounded by three contextual 
conditions. First, facilitator expertise: the game's affective empathy mechanisms operate within 
a structured educational context and require a facilitator who possesses both substantive 
knowledge of the conflict and pedagogical expertise in game-based learning. Without this dual 
competence, the productive tension between player agency and historical constraint may 
generate distress rather than understanding. Second, participant readiness: the game is 
appropriate for participants aged approximately 16 and above, who possess the cognitive and 
emotional capacities for sustained engagement with moral ambiguity. Third, trauma proximity: 
in educational settings where participants have direct personal or familial connection to the 
Troubles, educators must assess the risk that the game's affective demands may re-activate 
personal trauma rather than producing the historical reflection the design intends. 
This concern is empirically grounded: the companion study found that participants with 
greater proximity to the conflict — predominantly UK respondents — showed notably higher 
willingness to justify political violence than international participants, suggesting that 
geographic and biographical proximity fundamentally shapes moral engagement with the 
simulation [7]. The study cautions, however, that with fewer than 5% of participants from 
Ireland and Northern Ireland itself, these proximity effects remain provisional: the voices most 
central to the conflict are precisely those most underrepresented in the existing empirical 
evidence base. Future studies should prioritise recruiting from affected communities in 
Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. 
Finally, the paper acknowledges the methodological limitations of expert elicitation data 
drawn from the game's own designer and artist. While this data provides valuable insight into

6. Conclusions 
This paper has argued that The Troubles: Shadow War in Northern Ireland operationalises 
affective historical empathy through three interlocking design dimensions — narrative, visual, 
and material — each activating documented psychological processes that produce the felt 
emotional connection to historical actors and events that cognitive engagement alone cannot 
achieve. The analysis extends Ambrosio's framework for historically-structured boardgames 
[2] by specifying the affective mechanisms activated by each design layer, advancing the state 
of the art in HSBG scholarship beyond documentation of cognitive outcomes toward a 
principled account of affective ones. 
The paper's central finding is that ludonarrative consonance — the purposeful alignment of 
procedural mechanics and historical narrative — is the necessary condition for this affective 
engagement. When what players do (mechanics) is authentically consonant with what the game 
means (historical narrative), players do not merely learn about the Troubles; they experience 
the structural constraints, moral costs, and human weight of a conflict that claims 
approximately 3,500 documented lives. Preliminary empirical support for this claim is 
provided by the companion study's "empathy gap" finding — that participants could empathise 
with those involved in political violence without endorsing their actions — which is precisely 
the affective–cognitive distinction the design intends to cultivate [7]. 
HSBGs thus represent a form of pedagogical possibility unavailable through conventional 
approaches — not because they replace the factual knowledge that cognitive engagement 
provides, but because they add the affective dimension that, as Endacott and Brooks [1] 
establish, is essential to genuinely historical empathy. Through co-constructed interactive 
storytelling, historically-structured boardgames transform history from a curriculum subject 
into a contested field that requires players to engage ethically with the past — providing 
insights beyond what textbooks can provide, and demanding emotional as well as intellectual 
response. This article's contribution to the state of the art is to specify — at the level of design 
— the conditions under which that emotional response becomes pedagogically reliable.
