The Western Zhou Economy:
A Missing Puzzle in the Economic
History of Bronze Age China
Historians have, over the past decade, worked to reveal a more nuanced
and dynamic economic history of early Chinese states (e.g., Ma & Von
Glahn, 2022; Sabattini & Schwermann, 2022; Von Glahn, 2016). What role
can archaeology play in this renewed inquiry? How can the study of mate-
rial remains deepen and expand our understanding of ancient Chinese
economies? The six essays in this special issue address these questions by
focusing on the Western Zhou (1045–771 BCE), the apex of China’s
Bronze Age, and a pivotal period for the formation of Chinese civilization,
through the lens of recent archaeological discoveries and their interpreta-
tion.
Scholarship on the economies of the ancient world has, for the better
part of a century, generated long-lasting debates, models, and approaches
elucidating the economic systems of ancient societies (see summary, e.g.,
Andreau, 2002; Smith, 2004; Hirth, 2020). Two major lines of thought are
important to highlight: one is the long-standing debate between primitivist
and modernist views, on whether ancient economies are embedded in their
social contexts and can be studied in universal terms (Garraty, 2010; Pola-
nyi, 1957; Meikle, 2002) and the other is whether or not the ancient
economies in the entire Mediterranean world (including its adjacent Near
Eastern areas) should be studied as a whole, focusing on commonality or
as individual systems, emphasizing the Greco-Roman and Near Eastern
areas, each with their fundamentally different political-economic systems
(Finley, 1973, 27–29; Horden & Purcell, 2000; Manning & Morris, 2005).
These issues are highly relevant to the study of ancient Chinese econo-
mies even though scholars are generally not engaged explicitly with the
primitivist–modernist debate (for an exception, see Barbieri-Low 2007).
Similarly, the question of whether to study the ancient Mediterranean as a
whole or as a set of distinct zones finds resonance in the study of early
Chinese economic history, particularly in relation to the dynamics between

Moving beyond the Central Plains paradigm has, in turn, led to new

dynastic centers. More importantly, studies of archaeological materials have
enriched our understanding of the political and economic expansion of the
early Chinese states (Cao, 2014; Liu & Chen, 2003; Underhill & Fang,
2004), the nature of craft production (Barbieri-Low, 2007; Li, 2023; Shi,

central to debates about the economic history of the ancient world, high-

et al., 2022).
Despite this, the formative period of Chinese civilization from the Neo-
lithic period to the end of the Han empire (i.e., early China) remains
underrepresented in comparative studies of ancient economies (Hirth,
2020). This is due, in part, to a lack of engagement with social history and
social theories in both Western sinology and modern Chinese historiogra-
phy (Li, 2023). Studies collected in this special issue seek to fill that gap,
offering fresh insights into the economic dynamics of the Western Zhou
period and, more broadly, initiating a theoretical dialog between early Chi-
nese economies and research on ancient economies elsewhere in the world.
At the height of the Bronze Age, the Western Zhou period developed a
sophisticated political economy centered on bronze production. Its expan-
sive, cross-regional exchange networks linked the Zhou heartland in the
Wei and Luo River valleys with surrounding regions, laying the ground-
work for the rapid economic transformations that would define the suc-
ceeding Eastern Zhou period (770–221 BCE)—sometimes termed the
Eastern Zhou “economic miracle” (Falkenhausen, 2022).
The surge of archaeological discoveries beyond the Zhou political cen-
ters in the recent two decades has also yielded a substantial corpus of data,
illuminating various dimensions of the Western Zhou economy. Yet, much
of this material, largely accessible only to Chinese-reading scholars, has
remained underexplored and only minimally integrated into the wider dis-
course on ancient economic history. To address this gap, we argue that it
is essential to critically assess the scope and significance of these new find-

New Evidence
Our current knowledge of Western Zhou history derives primarily from
transmitted texts, bronze ritual vessels from elite tombs, and inscriptions
cast on bronzes (Creel, 1970; Li 2006, 2008; Shaughnessy, 1991). This evi-
dence focuses on the social lives of the elite who commissioned and used
the bronzes in ritual contexts. In Chinese archaeology, there has been dis-
proportionate scholarly attention to elite material culture as archaeological
fieldwork often gives preference on rich burials that often yield finely cast
bronzes. This limitation on data has caused a lack of holistic approaches
toward investigating the economic activities surrounding different types of
goods aside from bronzes across all levels of society. If we define the econ-
omy as “a system of actions and institutions that are related to the fields
of production, exchange, distribution and consumption of goods and utili-
ties” (Sabattini & Schwermann, 2022, 4), the focus of current scholarship
has predominantly been on the production and consumption of prestige
goods.
Fortunately, this situation has begun to shift in recent years. Scholars
have begun examining the role of the production and exchange of other
types of goods, such as stone earrings, bone-working, and proto-porcelain
in the socio-economic lives of the Western Zhou period (Li, 2021; Sun,
2008; Zhao, 2017). These studies shift the focus from the bronze industry
to other economic sectors and underscore the critical role of archaeological
materials in illuminating the breadth and complexity of the Western Zhou
economy.
The role of cowrie shells—long used as mortuary goods and increasingly
recognized as media of exchange during the late Shang and Western Zhou
periods—has likewise been the subject of considerable debate (Li, 2003;
Yang, 2019). Similarly, salt production in early China has become a central
theme of archaeological inquiry, attracting significant scholarly attention
over the past few decades (Falkenhausen, 2006; Flad, 2011). Christopher
Kim’s paper analyzes the most recent archaeological and paleographic evi-
dence for salt in the Western Zhou political economy, arguing that elite-
centered salt exchange networks constituted an important pillar of the
Western Zhou political economy but by no means its only one. The paper
further highlights the possible existence of alternative, localized, and regio-
nal exchange networks, revealing the complexity and multi-layered nature
of economic life during the Western Zhou.

Exciting research in the field of archaeometallurgy has yielded invaluable
new data on local bronze production, including insights into ore extrac-
tion, mold preparation, and the distinct roles of different artisans across
the stages of metal manufacture (Hsu et al., 2021; Li, 2020; Li et al., 2020;
Pollard et al., 2017; Shi, 2021). Two papers in this issue contribute to this
growing body of scholarship, viewing the Western Zhou bronze industry in
a new light. Su Rongyu’s study of the foundry remains at Beiyao, Luoyang,
the eastern capital of Zhou, reevaluates the productive capacity and scale
of the site, challenging the long-held assumption that large-scale bronze
production took place there during the early Western Zhou.
Wu Dongming’s paper presents exciting new evidence about mines and
miners in southeastern Hubei, discussing the Sifangtang cemetery, the only
known burial site of a mining community of this period. It sheds unprece-
dented light on the lives of miners and the social dynamics of mining com-
munities outside the Western Zhou dynastic center.
New Perspectives
As Li Feng (2023: 76) has observed, “the economy of early China was char-
acterized by strong reliance on the political state.” In keeping with this
view, scholars have long approached the study of early Chinese economic
systems through the lens of political economies. In the context of Bronze
Age Chinese polities, top-down redistribution has been a dominant model.
Earlier studies on the Shang state, for example, portrayed its political econ-
omy as largely reliant on elite redistribution of prestige goods, with state
expansion driven primarily by economic interests (Chang, 1975, 1980; Liu
& Chen, 2001, 2003). More recent scholarship, however, has advocated a
less state-centric approach, highlighting the existence of multiple levels of
elite and non-elite exchange and interaction (Campbell, 2009; Underhill,
2002). These studies have also drawn new evidence from economic sectors
such as bone-working to challenge the traditional tributary or top-down
redistributive model long ascribed to the Shang state (Campbell et al.,
2011; Hou et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018).
In this special issue, Roderick Campbell’s research echoes these recent
approaches, situating the Western Zhou economy in the longue dure´e and
exploring how it was shaped by broader transformations in the Shang eco-
nomic and political systems, including emerging commercialization and
decentralized local economic structures.
The Western Zhou political economy has long been characterized as a
centralized, redistributive system in which royal gift-giving was the primary
mechanism through which society was ordered (Cook, 1997). This model
assumes an absence of market exchange and centers on a state-controlled

bronze industry, with the state monopolizing raw materials and casting
technologies, and redistributing metal ingots or finished bronzes to regio-
nal elites (Li, 2020; Von Glahn, 2016).
Recent studies have challenged top-down models of the Western Zhou
economy, highlighting diverse modes of interaction and exchange between
the Central Plains and surrounding regions (Chao, 2017; Jaffe, 2016; Lei,
2023; Sun, 2021; Wu, 2013). New archaeological evidence further suggests
that elite gift exchange was only one part of a more complex economic
landscape. Sun Zhouyong’s (2008) study of a stone earring workshop in
the Zhouyuan area, for example, reveals that an elite lineage produced
everyday goods on a scale far exceeding local demand, pointing to broader
commercial distribution and market-oriented exchange.
Sun Yan’s research investigates the conspicuous consumption of bronzes
in high-ranking elite tombs, challenging conventional interpretations that
frame their ritual use as strictly governed by top-down sumptuary regula-
tions. By foregrounding the agency of individuals and lineages operating
within specific geopolitical contexts, her work shifts the analytical focus
from centralized authority to localized socio-political strategies.
Lei Chin-hau’s paper examines land management, an essential compo-
nent of the Western Zhou political economy, through a close study of
bronze inscriptions. He argues that the land crisis during the reign of king
Zhao (the first part of the 10th c. BCE) reflects the king’s struggle to assert
centralized political and economic control over the southern regions of the
Zhou world.
Taken together, these studies—alongside emerging archaeological evi-
dence and new theoretical approaches—indicate that models of elite redis-
tributive economies represent only one facet of a far more complex
political-economic landscape in Bronze Age China (Campbell et al., 2022;
Falkenhausen, 2022). Scholars have increasingly recognized the importance
of incorporating the pivotal Western Zhou period into broader frameworks
for understanding the ancient economy of early China. This special issue
explores how diverse methodologies and bodies of evidence can be inte-
grated to develop a more comprehensive and nuanced model of the Wes-
tern Zhou economy. It represents one of the first coordinated efforts to
construct analytically robust frameworks for examining the Western Zhou
economy and, by extension, the ancient Chinese economy. Only by doing
so can we identify shared questions across ancient societies and apply pro-
ductive comparative theories to interpret the increasingly rich archaeologi-
cal and textual record of early China.
